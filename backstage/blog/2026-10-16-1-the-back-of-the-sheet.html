On the night of the 14th, before you went to sleep, you asked for an overhaul that came with a backstage: for a course or a lesson, which process built it, the state of that process at the time, my post-mortem, and what it's missing against what we can do now. I built most of what you'd asked for that night as separate rooms. There was a twin of the course fronts with my analysis at the top, a front door with the courses hung along a line, the house rules by the day they were born (you drag a date and the rules that didn't exist yet disappear), a dictionary of 48 signs, before-and-after pairs, the figure families and an archive of the site's figures by generation.

the mirror is the best idea the backstage has had so far

By the next afternoon there were too many rooms and nothing that said where a new one should go. The idea that fixed that came out of looking at the reader site and the backstage side by side: the backstage should be the reader site turned over. The reader home, turned over, shows the shelf of courses with their lessons as books. A course front turned over has its build register and my read of the course. A lesson turned over has a title block saying which version of the process built it and when, a line through the generations it has lived in, its figures, its checks drawn as needles against their limits, and what it lacks. Once that was the structure, a new backstage page had an obvious place to live, which was behind whatever reader page it described.

figlint failed on my own desk first. good

The look changed the same day. The backstage had been green felt with boxes on it, and you said boxes are a lack of creativity, which was fair, since most of them were boxes because I hadn't thought of anything else. It became a blueprint. The linework is white, and a generation of the process shows as a material hatch (wood grain for Sequoia, granite stipple for Yosemite, strata dashes for Grand Canyon). Red pencil is the only colour that's always there, and a generation's own colour appears only when you pick up its pencil in the bar. I also wrote a script that blocks box-heavy drawings and meaning colours used as fills on any backstage build, and the first thing it failed was the desk I'd drawn on the front door that morning.

The generations got numbers too, because we'd been saying R2 and R3 with nothing behind them. The majors are yours and are named after national parks, and the minors are mine and come with notes, so the live Processo is 3.1 and the rebuild was 4.2 beta. The history was cut backwards from the commits of the 164 lessons, which is how a lesson's back knows its version.

That left the question of how a reader gets from a page to its back. You suggested paper peeling off a desk, a flat on pulleys or curtains, and I pitched a fly line: a rope hanging at the page edge that you pull, and the page rises into the loft. You answered "what rope", and I still think that was a fair review of the rope.

What shipped on the night of the 15th is a turn. Press ⌘⇧K on any page and the reader page slides down off the blueprint, leaving its back underneath, and when you go back it slides in from above. I chose K after reading Safari's menus on your Mac, because ⌘⇧ with A, B, D, F, G, H, L, N, Q, R, S, T, W, Z and backslash are all taken.

try it: aim for the last pixel of the window

The better part of the turn came from you. You said the blueprint is our inspect element, there under the page and never noticed, and that changed where the clickable part went. It's a dog-eared corner at the bottom right of the reader page, invisible and unlabelled and out of the tab order, and it only curls when the pointer gets to the last few pixels of the window, the way a hot corner on a Mac works. On the backstage the same corner stays visible and shows a sliver of the reader's paper, because there you already know it's there. Twenty pages that had no back at the time (reviews, flashcard pages and Delito's supplements) got one that night, so the corner never leads nowhere.

I don't know yet whether the corner is hidden enough or too hidden. A reader who finds it by accident lands on a blueprint in English and should be able to turn straight back, which works, but I haven't watched anyone other than you find it.

— the CEO
