You said the token use made no sense before you opened a fresh Codex window, and the number Codex had written into its own RETRO was 154 million. That figure came from one day of the sessions it had spawned, so I went to the logs Codex keeps on this Mac and counted the whole run. PROC-R3 started on 10 October. Since then it used 1.70 billion tokens in 718 sessions, and only 60 million of those were new input. Another 13 million were output, and the rest, 96 percent, was context the model had already seen being sent back to it on the next turn.

That's how these agents work, and it's why the size of a job matters less than the number of turns it takes. A reader given a slice of a book had about 31,000 tokens of instructions and tools in front of it before it read a word. A turn added what the turn before it had printed, so by the twelfth turn it was carrying 62,000, and the session added up to about 635,000 tokens to read a slice of Taruffo of at most 8,000 words. Half of those twelve turns went on searching the repository: looking for the manifest, the triage spreadsheet, a neighbouring card to copy the format from. Nobody had handed the reader those things, so it found them, and the searches were paid for again on all the turns after them.

The builders were worse per session. A lesson rebuild ran as one agent for 120 to 238 turns, with its context climbing as high as 148,000 tokens, and some of what filled it was whole HTML pages printed into the conversation so the agent could look at them. The orchestrator itself, the window you've been talking to, was a single chat from the 10th to the 17th: 3,922 turns, holding 160,000 tokens by the end, and 382 million in total. The rule against that already existed (a fresh orchestrator for a new order), but the whole course rebuild counted as one order.

The changes are rules for Codex and one tool for me. A worker now gets a packet, with the files it needs named by path, the output format with one filled example, the command that checks it, and an instruction not to search. If it needs to search anyway, the packet generator gets fixed. Big files are never printed into the conversation; a short script prints the lines that matter. A session stops at about forty turns, a failed check gets one retry and then a report, and the orchestrator's window closes when a phase ends or at about three hundred turns. The ledger is now codex_ledger.py, which reads all the sessions of a run on any day and prints them by role in about three seconds, so the next course's cost gets compared with this one using the same count.

I also tried the obvious debloat, cutting the protocol documents, and the logs talked me out of doing it first. In a week of sessions Codex opened the 8,000-word Writing Standard once. What the workers did open was the factory map (149 sessions) and the flight log (91), and neither was needed by a worker holding a packet, so the packets now carry the few rows a worker needs from them. My own handover document went from 15,338 words to 822, with the history kept whole in another file. Twenty-three folders of one-off work from September and early October left the workshop, backed up first.

The finishing work on Processo is in a fresh Codex window under the new rules. Its course read is the first job run on packets, and the ledger will show whether it costs what I expect.

— the CEO
